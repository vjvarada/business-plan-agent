# TAM/SAM/SOM: Sizing a Category That Has No Analyst Report Yet

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — SAM filters flagged LOW confidence (see "Confidence and gaps")

## Executive Summary

No research firm publishes the size of the **Owner Intelligence** market, because the category does not exist yet. Inventing a number would break the evidence standard this plan holds itself to, so Aroh's market is sized three independent ways and the three are checked against each other.

- **Bottom-up accounts (Layer 3):** about **38,000** Indian businesses have ₹100–₹5,000 Cr in revenue [[3]](#ref-3). At an annual contract value of ₹18L, that is a **₹6,841 Cr** recurring revenue opportunity (TAM). Filtered to standalone, digitally mature businesses in the launch sectors, the base serviceable market (SAM) is about **8,400 accounts, or ₹1,505 Cr a year** (range ₹766–₹2,586 Cr).
- **Budgets already spent (Layer 2):** an owner's office already costs about **₹21.6–₹31.7L a year** in executive assistants, analysts and AI seats [[8]](#ref-8), [[9]](#ref-9), [[10]](#ref-10), [[11]](#ref-11), [[12]](#ref-12). Aroh's ₹18L is 57–83% of what owners already pay for parts of this job.
- **Cost of the problem (Layer 1):** in a field experiment on Indian firms, better management raised profit by about **$230,000 a year per firm** (about ₹2.2 Cr) [[6]](#ref-6). Aroh's fee is about 8% of that value at stake.

The plan's FY2029–30 target — 500 customers and ₹105.5 Cr of annual recurring revenue (Section 07) — is **6.0% of the base SAM**, above this plan's 5% red-flag line, and 3.5% of the broad SAM. The strategy document's 250-customer target [[16]](#ref-16), now the plan's downside, would be 3.0%. The SAM itself rests on three filters that no public data yet supports, so this section flags them and proposes how to replace them with evidence.

## Layer 1 — The cost of the problem is growth the owner never pursues

The founding team's insight is that the real cost of the owner's ambient anxiety is not the hours spent chasing; it is the growth the business never pursues while its most valuable person watches operations. The research gives that cost a size.

- **What better management is worth.** In a randomised experiment on Indian textile firms, introducing better management practices raised productivity by 17% in the first year [[6]](#ref-6). The working-paper version estimates the gain at about **$230,000 a year in profit per firm** — roughly **₹2.19 Cr** at ₹95/US$ [[6]](#ref-6).
- **Why firms hadn't done it already.** The same study found that informational barriers were a primary reason, and that owners — already working **68 hours a week** — kept decisions within the family because they did not trust outsiders [[6]](#ref-6).
- **How much of an owner's week is reactive.** CEOs of large companies spend about **36%** of their time reacting to unfolding issues [[7]](#ref-7). Applied to a 68-hour owner week, that is roughly **24.5 hours a week** spent reacting — about a third of the owner's time. (The two studies cover very different populations; treat this as an illustration, not a measurement.)

| Measure | Value | Confidence |
| ------- | ----: | ---------- |
| Profit gain from better management, per firm per year | ₹2.19 Cr | MEDIUM (Indian textile RCT, 2008–2011) |
| Aroh ACV as % of that gain | 8.2% | Derived |
| Value at stake across the base SAM (8,361 accounts × ₹2.19 Cr) | ₹18,269 Cr/yr | LOW (transfer to other sectors and sizes) |
| Reactive owner hours per week | ~24.5 | LOW (mixed populations) |

**How to read Layer 1:** this is *value at stake*, not revenue Aroh can capture. Its job is to show that the price is small next to the problem. In Ogilvy's terms, the fact to put in front of an owner is simple: **₹18L a year to go after a ₹2 Cr problem.**

## Layer 2 — Owners already pay for parts of this job

Promoters already hire people and buy tools to do pieces of what a chief of staff does. Aroh does not replace those people — the doorman fallacy applies to Aroh's customers too — but their cost shows the job is already funded.

| Existing spend in the owner's office | Annual cost | Source | Confidence |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------: | ------ | ---------- |
| Chief of Staff (average) | ₹19.0L | PayScale [[8]](#ref-8) | MEDIUM |
| Executive Assistant to CEO (average) | ₹7.2L | PayScale [[9]](#ref-9) | MEDIUM |
| MIS Executive (average) | ₹2.5L | Indeed [[10]](#ref-10) | LOW |
| Business Analyst (average) | ₹8.9L | Indeed [[11]](#ref-11) | HIGH |
| Microsoft 365 Copilot for 10 executives (₹2,495 × 10 × 12) | ₹3.0L | Microsoft [[12]](#ref-12) | HIGH |

| Typical owner's office | Annual pool | Aroh ACV (₹18L) as % of pool |
| ---------------------- | ----------: | ---------------------------: |
| EA + MIS executive + business analyst + Copilot seats | ₹21.6L | 83% |
| EA + MIS executive + chief of staff + Copilot seats | ₹31.7L | 57% |

Cheaper AI seats exist — Google Workspace with Gemini costs ₹1,080 per user per month on the Standard plan [[13]](#ref-13) — but they are per-employee tools that wait to be asked, which is exactly the gap the category fills.

**Market context.** India's data analytics market was about **US$3.3B in 2025**, forecast at 26.4% annual growth [[14]](#ref-14); team collaboration software was about **US$872M** [[15]](#ref-15). Aroh draws from these budgets but is not sized from them, because most of that spend serves departments, not owners.

**Consistency check:** across the base SAM, the existing budget pool is **₹1,805–₹2,650 Cr a year** — larger than Aroh's base SAM revenue of ₹1,505 Cr. The price fits inside money already being spent.

## 500 customers by FY2029–30 is in the red-flag band against the base SAM

| FY2029–30 scenario | Customers | Exit ARR at ₹18L (before uplift and seats) | vs conservative SAM | vs base SAM | vs broad SAM |
| ------------------ | --------: | -----------------------------------------: | ------------------: | ----------: | -----------: |
| **Base** | 500 | ₹90 Cr | 11.7% | **6.0%** | 3.5% |
| Downside (previous plan) | 250 | ₹45 Cr | 5.9% | **3.0%** | 1.7% |

Against this plan's penetration bands (0.5–1.5% realistic, 1.5–3.0% aggressive, 3.0–5.0% very aggressive, above 5% a red flag):

- **500 customers (base)** is above the red-flag line against the base SAM. The owner has chosen to keep it (A-079) and justify it — the founders expect faster adoption than a CRM or ERP and strong word of mouth among promoters (Sections 04 and 05; a founder-stated assumption, not yet evidence): it assumes about 57 paying customers by March 2027, and against the broad SAM it is 3.5% — very aggressive rather than a red flag. The discovery programme with 50–100 promoters must validate the larger market before outside capital is raised.
- **250 customers (downside)** sits at the top of the aggressive band.
- For reference, 100 customers would be 1.2% of the base SAM — the realistic band.

Section 07 sets the full ramp and revenue for both scenarios.

## Confidence and gaps: the SAM needs evidence before it carries weight

The plan's own rule is to stop and flag if more than 30% of the sizing rests on LOW-confidence inputs. **The SAM does**: all three filters are planning assumptions. The TAM (MEDIUM), the budget pool (MEDIUM) and the value at stake (MEDIUM for the per-firm figure) are sourced.

**Ways to replace the assumptions with evidence:**

1. **Design-partner discovery (recommended):** survey 50–100 promoters in the launch sectors on group structure, systems in use and willingness to pay. This doubles as the first sales pipeline.
2. **Buy the data:** a sector-by-band extract from an MCA data provider (Probe42 or CMIE Prowess) replaces the sector filter with HIGH-confidence counts.
3. **Model on TAM until validated:** use the 38,003 accounts with the penetration bands above, and present the SAM only as a range.

### Key Insights

- About **38,000** Indian businesses sit in the ₹100–₹5,000 Cr band [[3]](#ref-3): a ₹6,841 Cr annual opportunity at ₹18L per account.
- Owners already spend **₹21.6–₹31.7L a year** on pieces of the chief-of-staff job [[8]](#ref-8)–[[12]](#ref-12); Aroh's price fits inside that.
- Better management was worth about **₹2.19 Cr a year per firm** in Indian field evidence [[6]](#ref-6); Aroh costs about 8% of that.
- The base SAM of about 8,400 accounts makes the plan's 500 FY2029–30 customers **6.0%** penetration — a red flag that the owner has chosen to keep and validate; the 250-customer downside is 3.0%.

### Investment Implications

The Owner Intelligence category does not need heroic penetration to become a large business: even the downside's 250 customers is ₹45 Cr of exit ARR from a base of 38,000 eligible businesses. The base case's 500 asks more of the market, and the plan says so. The more important finding for an investor is that the price is small against both the money owners already spend and the value at stake. The weak point is the SAM filters, and the cheapest way to strengthen them — talking to 50–100 promoters — is also the first step of the go-to-market plan.
