# 00 — Category Design: Owner Intelligence

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh (built on DEWiN + Metorite building blocks)
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

Indian businesses have never had more systems, and their owners have never had less peace of mind. The ERP holds the receivables, the CRM holds the pipeline, the inbox holds the commitments and a dozen WhatsApp groups hold everything else. Each system knows its own slice. None of them tells the owner what matters across all of them — so the owner's own head remains the place where the business is finally joined up.

That is the problem Aroh names, and it is a growing one. Two-thirds of Indian CEOs surveyed by PwC say they are concerned about keeping pace with technology and AI, against 42% globally [[1]](#s00-ref-1). Indian companies are deploying AI quickly — 47% already have several GenAI use cases in production [[2]](#s00-ref-2) — yet only 4% say their enterprise data is fully ready for AI at scale [[3]](#s00-ref-3). More tools have not produced more certainty.

The stakes reach beyond any one company. The IMF expects India's economy to pass **$6T in 2030** [[12]](#s00-ref-12), and getting there quickly means more Indian businesses growing from mid-sized to large. Research on Indian firms finds their owners already working 68-hour weeks and unwilling to delegate without better information [[13]](#s00-ref-13). For India's businesses to grow at the pace the country needs, their owners must resolve their business anxiety — through an intelligent understanding of their own business — and turn their attention to growth.

Aroh creates a new category to close that gap: **Owner Intelligence** — software that does for the owner what a good chief of staff does. It is not a chatbot, a dashboard or a copilot for one employee. It watches the whole business, tells the owner what deserves attention, explains why with the source attached, and follows the work through until it is done. The feeling it sells is **control that brings calm**.

---

## The Big Idea

> **Wake up knowing what matters, why it matters, who needs to act — and what Aroh has already done about it.**

The promise is specific enough to check every morning. At 07:30 the owner either receives the three things that deserve attention today, or does not. At 18:30 Aroh reports which actions closed and which need attention tomorrow. A promise the customer can test daily is a promise the company has to keep — and that is what makes it believable.

**The same idea, at national scale:** India's businesses grow faster when their owners stop carrying the whole business in their heads. Every owner who hands the watching to Owner Intelligence gets back the attention that growth requires — and India's path to $6T [[12]](#s00-ref-12) runs through thousands of those owners.

## The Problem We Name

Owners of growing Indian businesses do not lack data. They lack confidence that nothing material is slipping between systems, functions and people. The anxiety shows up as six recurring questions, each of which hides an operational failure [[10]](#s00-ref-10):

| What the owner asks | The operational failure underneath |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| "What am I missing?" | Exception invisibility |
| "Why did nobody tell me?" | Information latency |
| "Did anyone actually do it?" | Follow-through risk |
| "Whose version is right?" | Cross-system reconciliation |
| "Why do I have to chase this?" | Management overhead |
| "Can I trust the number?" | Provenance and auditability |

### The real cost: the most valuable person in the business is working on the wrong things

Past a certain size, the systems that run a business need constant tuning, follow-up and oversight to keep working. That work flows upward to the one person who can see across all of them: the owner. Over time the owner comes to feel they must be on top of every information flow in the company. When information stops reaching them, they grow anxious — not about a known problem, but about the **unknown unknowns** somewhere inside their own organisation [[11]](#s00-ref-11).

That ambient anxiety has a price. It pulls the person whose attention is worth the most into day-to-day operations, away from strategy, from spotting new opportunities and from chasing growth. The cost of the problem is not the hours spent chasing follow-ups. It is the growth the business never pursues because its owner was busy checking that nothing had broken.

The market evidence points the same way. Indian software spending is forecast to rise 17.6% to $24.7B in 2026 [[4]](#s00-ref-4); 69% of Indian businesses plan to increase AI investment and 73% report some measurable return [[3]](#s00-ref-3). Spending on tools is rising faster than the owner's ability to see across them. The missing piece is not another system. It is someone — or something — whose job is to watch all of them on the owner's behalf.

## The Enemy — the owner's head as the integration layer

Aroh's enemy is not Microsoft, SAP, Salesforce or Zoho. Those are systems the customer already owns, and Aroh is more valuable *because* they all coexist. The enemy is a behaviour: **the owner acting as the integration layer** — personally joining the receivables report to the sales call to the WhatsApp message from the plant, every day, from memory.

This behaviour survives for reasons that look irrational on a spreadsheet but are perfectly sensible to the owner:

- **It feels safe.** Checking personally is the one method nobody can be blamed for.
- **It feels like control.** Being the person who knows everything is part of the owner's authority.
- **Delegation has failed before.** Dashboards were built and stopped being read; managers summarised and left things out.

Any category that asks the owner to give up this role must first give back the feeling it provides — certainty — before asking for the time it costs.

## From → To

| Old way | New way, with Owner Intelligence |
| ------- | -------------------------------- |
| The owner wakes up asking "what am I missing?" | Aroh opens at 07:30 with the three things that matter today |
| Updates arrive whenever someone remembers | Exceptions surface the moment a threshold is crossed |
| The owner chases follow-ups by phone and WhatsApp | Aroh closes the loop and reports back at 18:30 |
| Five systems, five versions of the number | One answer, with its source shown |
| Dashboards the owner has to read | A brief that reads the dashboards for them |
| Delegation depends on memory | Every commitment is tracked through to its outcome |

## Category Name — Owner Intelligence

**Why this name:**

- **It borrows a frame buyers already know and points it somewhere new.** Every promoter has heard of business intelligence. Business intelligence served analysts with dashboards; Owner Intelligence serves the owner with priorities, answers and follow-through. A familiar frame lowers the cost of learning a new category — Sutherland's point that changing how something is perceived can be worth as much as changing what it is [[6]](#s00-ref-6).
- **It names the buyer.** The product works for one person, the owner — not for every employee.
- **It names the outcome.** Intelligence here means understanding the business well enough to act: the intelligent understanding owners need to move from worry to growth.
- **It is unclaimed.** A label search on 24 October 2026 found no product or category called Owner Intelligence [[14]](#s00-ref-14). The nearest name, Owner.com, is a US restaurant-marketing platform in a different market.

**From business intelligence to Owner Intelligence:**

| Business intelligence | Owner Intelligence |
| --------------------- | ------------------ |
| Built for analysts | Built for the owner |
| Dashboards you have to read | A brief that reads them for you |
| Reports what happened | Says what matters, why, and what has already been done |
| Waits to be opened | Speaks first at 07:30, stays quiet unless something matters |
| Stops at insight | Follows through to action, with the owner's approval |

**Names considered and rejected:**

| Candidate | Why it was rejected |
| --------- | ------------------- |
| AI Chief of Staff | Buyers understand it instantly, but at least 11 products already use it — including Bond (YC), WorkBoard's agent, Aron and Fambot — and press coverage now applies it to Anthropic's Claude Duet [[14]](#s00-ref-14). A contested label cannot be owned. Aroh keeps the phrase only to explain what it does: it works like a chief of staff. |
| Company Operating System | "OS" is an overused label; describes the platform, not the outcome |
| Business Nervous System | Essentially unclaimed and vivid, but no buyer searches for it [[14]](#s00-ref-14) |
| Promoter Intelligence | Unclaimed and unmistakably Indian, but "promoter" reads as "event promoter" to many global investors |
| Owner's Command Centre | Too narrow; describes a screen rather than a role |
| Copilot | A Microsoft product name priced per employee [[5]](#s00-ref-5); places Aroh inside someone else's category |
| AI assistant / AI agent | Generic; invites comparison on features and price |

**The risk in the name — "intelligence" can sound like dashboards.** If buyers hear Owner Intelligence as "BI for owners", Aroh is pulled back into the dashboard category it exists to leave. The defence is the job description: Owner Intelligence does what a good chief of staff does — observes, filters, prepares and acts within a mandate. The doorman fallacy applies here too [[6]](#s00-ref-6): a human chief of staff is not only a schedule-keeper, but also offers discretion, judgment and loyalty. Aroh earns the category by keeping those — approval before any sensitive action, the source attached to every claim, and autonomy the owner sets, never assumes.

## Point of View

Indian business was digitised one department at a time. Accounts moved to Tally or an ERP. Sales moved to a CRM. Projects moved to a tracker. Every team got a WhatsApp group. Each system made its own department more efficient. None was built to tell the owner what matters across all of them.

So the owner became the integration layer. The promoter of a ₹500 Cr manufacturer still finds out about a slipping customer commitment from a phone call, reconciles two versions of the receivables number in a review meeting, and chases the same follow-up three times on WhatsApp. The systems are modern. The way the owner finds out is not.

The industry's answer has been more software: more dashboards, more reports, and now a copilot for every employee. Each of these adds information. None of them takes responsibility. A dashboard waits to be read. A copilot waits to be asked. The owner does not need another thing to check; they need something that checks for them.

We believe a new kind of software is needed — **Owner Intelligence**. It works for one person, the owner, and across every system the business already uses. It does four things a good chief of staff does:

1. **Observes** — watches the ERP, CRM, email, calendar and authorised messaging for what has changed.
2. **Recommends** — decides what deserves the owner's attention today, and explains why with the source attached.
3. **Prepares** — drafts the message, the follow-up or the record update.
4. **Acts, with permission** — sends, updates and schedules within limits the owner sets, and asks first for anything sensitive.

Then it closes the loop: it reports what happened, what is still open and who is holding it.

The category's value is not hours saved, though hours are saved. It is the change in how running the business *feels*: from carrying everything in one head to knowing that someone is watching. Control, and the calm that comes with it.

There is a national stake in this too. India's next milestone — a $6T economy, which the IMF expects in 2030 [[12]](#s00-ref-12) — depends on businesses that keep growing instead of stalling at the size one person can watch. The research on Indian firms is clear about the bottleneck: owners stretched to 68-hour weeks, holding decisions close because they cannot see enough to let go [[13]](#s00-ref-13). Better information is what made delegation possible in that research. Owner Intelligence makes that information flow the default.

This category is arriving now for three reasons. AI that can read, reason and draft across messy business data has become cheap enough to run every day for every business. Indian companies are adopting AI faster than their data is becoming ready for it [[3]](#s00-ref-3). And owners of growing companies are saying, in survey after survey, that keeping pace is what worries them [[1]](#s00-ref-1).

The category leader will be the company that earns the owner's trust first — by being right about what matters, showing its sources and never acting beyond its mandate. Aroh intends to be that company.

## The Psychological Job — control that brings calm

Aroh's functional job is to detect exceptions and follow work through. Its psychological job is larger: **to let the owner stop carrying the whole business in their head.**

Sutherland's account of the Uber map makes the point [[6]](#s00-ref-6): the map did not make taxis arrive any sooner, it removed the uncertainty of waiting. Aroh does the same for the owner. The 07:30 brief does not make receivables come in faster; it removes the dread of not knowing which ones are at risk.

| Moment | What the owner feels | What makes it believable (control) |
| ------ | -------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 07:30 brief | "I know what matters today" | Three items, ranked, each with its source |
| During the day | "It will tell me if something breaks" | Silence unless a threshold is crossed |
| Approval request | "Nothing happens without me" | An approval card before any sensitive action |
| 18:30 close-out | "Nothing fell through the cracks" | Count of actions started, completed and still open |

**Implications for the rest of the plan:**

- **Copy leads with calm and proves it with control.** The headline benefit is never "productivity".
- **Price is a signal.** An organisation-level fee says "this runs your business". A per-seat utility price would say "this is a tool". (Detail in Section 07.)
- **Remove the buyer's risk of being blamed.** India hosting, approval gates, a paid pilot around one business loop and references from known promoters make Aroh the safe choice, not the brave one. (Detail in Section 06.)

## Who It Is For (and Not For)

**For:**

- **Economic buyer:** the promoter, managing director or CEO of an Indian business with roughly ₹100–₹5,000 Cr turnover. This range is a working hypothesis to test with early customers, not a published market definition [[10]](#s00-ref-10).
- **Power users:** the COO, CFO and executive office — around 10 executive users per organisation [[8]](#s00-ref-8).
- **Launch sectors:** manufacturing and industrial, distribution and logistics, and B2B services, where a missed exception has a direct cash or delivery cost.
- **Geography:** India, with India-hosted data.

**Not for:**

- Individual knowledge workers who want a personal assistant.
- Businesses that run entirely inside one vendor's suite and already have in-house AI teams.
- Financial services and healthcare at launch — the compliance load is heavier, and both can follow once the core is proven.
- Businesses with little digital footprint; Aroh needs systems to watch.

### Key Insights

- The enemy is a behaviour — the owner as integration layer — not a competitor, so Aroh adds value to systems customers already own.
- **Owner Intelligence** names the buyer and the outcome in two words a promoter can repeat, borrows the familiar "business intelligence" frame, and is unclaimed [[14]](#s00-ref-14).
- The felt benefit is calm; the proof is control: ranked briefs, sources, approvals and close-outs.
- AI adoption in India is outrunning data readiness (47% in production vs 4% data-ready) [[2]](#s00-ref-2), [[3]](#s00-ref-3) — the gap Aroh fills.
- Incumbents price AI per employee [[5]](#s00-ref-5); organisation-level Owner Intelligence is a different purchase with a different buyer.

### Investment Implications

An investor in Aroh is not buying a share of an existing software market; they are buying the chance to define a new one. If Play Bigger's finding holds — that category leaders take most of a category's value [[7]](#s00-ref-7) — the decisive question is not "how big is the market today" but "who names the category first and earns the owner's trust". Aroh starts with a clear enemy, a name buyers understand, product building blocks already live, and a promise customers can check every morning. The open risk is evidence: the POV must now be proven with paying design partners, which Sections 05 and 06 address.

# 01 — Market Drivers: Why the Owner Can No Longer Be the Integration Layer

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

The way Indian owners run their businesses — personally joining every system, report and WhatsApp thread — was workable when businesses were smaller and slower. It is breaking now, for reasons that arrived together in the last two years.

The first is the stakes. India's next stage of growth depends on its mid-sized businesses growing, and that growth depends on owners having the attention to pursue it: the IMF expects India to pass $6T in 2030 [[1]](#s01-ref-1), while research on Indian firms finds owners working 68-hour weeks and reluctant to delegate [[4]](#s01-ref-4). The second is that the tools have changed: the cost of running AI at a fixed level of quality is falling about 40× a year [[8]](#s01-ref-8), and frontier models can now run inside India [[15]](#s01-ref-15). The third is pressure: Indian companies have adopted AI faster than they have made their data ready [[13]](#s01-ref-13), [[9]](#s01-ref-9), and Indian CEOs worry about keeping pace more than their global peers [[10]](#s01-ref-10). The fourth is law: India's data protection rules, notified in November 2025, phase in through 2027 [[12]](#s01-ref-12) and turn informal information flows into a liability.

Together these make **Owner Intelligence** both possible and necessary. Each driver below is stated as a claim, followed by the evidence and what it means for the category.

---

## India's growth ambition now depends on owners who have time to grow

The IMF projects India's nominal GDP at $4.06T in 2026, crossing $5T in 2029 and **$6T in 2030** ($6.13T) [[1]](#s01-ref-1). Reaching that milestone on time — or sooner — depends on more Indian firms growing from small to large. Today, too few do:

- A 40-year-old US manufacturing plant employs almost **eight times** as many workers as one under five years old; surviving Indian plants show little growth with age, a gap that could lower manufacturing productivity by about 25% [[6]](#s01-ref-6).
- "Dwarfs" — firms with fewer than 100 workers that are more than 10 years old — make up more than half of India's organised manufacturing firms but contribute only 14% of its jobs and 8% of its productivity [[2]](#s01-ref-2).
- Medium enterprises are just **0.3%** of registered MSMEs (67,923 firms), yet earn about 40% of MSME export income [[3]](#s01-ref-3).

The research points to the owner's attention as one reason firms stall:

- **Owners are already stretched.** In a field study of Indian textile firms, owners worked an average of **68 hours a week**. Major decisions stayed within the owning family because owners did not trust outsiders, and firm size tracked the number of family members available to share control. Lack of information was a primary reason firms had not adopted better management; better information let owners delegate more, and better management raised productivity **17%** in the first year [[4]](#s01-ref-4).
- **Indian CEOs are pulled into operations.** Across 1,114 CEOs — 356 of them in India — **36%** of CEOs in lower-income countries (Brazil and India) behave in ways mismatched to what their firms need, against 5% in high-income countries. That mismatch accounts for up to 13% of the labour-productivity gap between richer and poorer countries [[5]](#s01-ref-5).

One caveat, stated plainly: economists dispute whether India has a "missing middle" in its firm-size distribution [[7]](#s01-ref-7). The problem Aroh addresses is not a hole in the distribution; it is firms that stop growing once their owner runs out of attention.

**What it means for the category:** the owner who cannot let go because they cannot see is not only a private frustration — it is a constraint on national growth. Owner Intelligence supplies the information flow that the research links to delegation. Aroh cannot move GDP on its own, but it is aimed at a constraint the evidence identifies, at the moment India most needs its businesses to grow.

## AI became cheap enough to watch every business, every day

Owner Intelligence reads, reasons and drafts across a business's systems all day. Two years ago that would have cost more than the job was worth. It no longer does.

- **The trend:** Epoch AI finds that the price of matching GPT-4's performance on PhD-level science questions fell **40× per year**, with declines ranging from 9× to 900× per year depending on the performance level [[8]](#s01-ref-8).
- **Aroh's own numbers:** Metorite's measured costs put a blended AI request at about **₹0.18** (at ₹95/US$), or roughly **₹944 per user per month** at average usage [[14]](#s01-ref-14). For a 10-user organisation that is about ₹9,435 a month — a small fraction of an organisation-level fee.

**What it means for the category:** the economics of "always watching" now work for a ₹100 Cr business, not only for a conglomerate. Cost will keep falling; the scarce asset is the owner's trust, not the compute.

## Indian companies adopted AI faster than they made their data ready

- **47%** of Indian organisations have several GenAI use cases live in production; **91%** say speed of deployment is the biggest factor in what they buy [[13]](#s01-ref-13).
- **69%** plan to increase AI investment and **73%** report some measurable return — yet only **4%** say their enterprise data is fully ready for AI at scale [[9]](#s01-ref-9).

**What it means for the category:** buyers want results quickly, from data that is messy and spread across systems. That favours a product that works *across* existing systems, shows its sources and goes live fast — not one that asks the customer to clean and centralise their data first.

## Indian CEOs feel the pressure to keep pace more than their global peers

PwC's 29th CEO Survey reports that **66%** of Indian CEOs are concerned about keeping pace with technology and AI, against **42%** of CEOs globally who name it as their top concern [[10]](#s01-ref-10). The India sample is small (around 50 CEOs) and the two figures do not measure exactly the same thing, but the direction is consistent with the other evidence: Indian business leaders feel AI pressure acutely.

**What it means for the category:** the anxiety Aroh addresses is already present and named by its buyers. Aroh does not have to create the worry; it has to resolve it.

## Frontier AI can now run inside India

On 29 September 2026, Amazon Bedrock made Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 available in India through geographic cross-Region inference across its Mumbai and Hyderabad Regions, so customers can keep inference processing within India [[15]](#s01-ref-15). Open-weight models have reached the same point: DeepSeek released V4 under the MIT licence in April 2026 [[17]](#s01-ref-17), so a frontier-class model can be run on Indian infrastructure by whoever chooses to host it.

**What it means for the category:** an owner can now be told, truthfully, that their business data is processed in India by frontier models. India hosting is a trust and procurement advantage — it removes a reason to say no. Aroh will present it that way, and not as a blanket legal requirement — data localisation obligations under Indian law need sector-by-sector legal review.

## Data protection law turns informal information flows into a liability

The Government of India notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules on **14 November 2025**, with an **18-month** phased compliance period [[12]](#s01-ref-12) — bringing the main obligations into force by around May 2027.

Today, much of what an owner knows arrives through forwarded WhatsApp messages, personal inboxes and spreadsheets passed between people. Under the new rules, that informal flow of personal data carries obligations around notice, consent and security.

**What it means for the category:** for years, the status quo was the safe choice and new software was the risky one. Data protection law reverses that. A governed system — with consent, permissions, approvals and an audit trail — becomes the option an owner cannot be blamed for choosing.

## Software budgets are rising, but they are spent per department and per employee

Gartner forecasts Indian software spending to grow **17.6% to $24.7B** in 2026 [[11]](#s01-ref-11). Most of that money buys departmental systems and per-employee tools: Microsoft 365 Copilot, for example, costs **₹2,495 per user per month**, paid yearly, on top of a qualifying Microsoft 365 licence [[16]](#s01-ref-16).

**What it means for the category:** budgets are growing, but nothing in them is bought *for the owner* or works *across* departments. Owner Intelligence is a new line item with a different buyer — the gap category creation exists to fill.

## Seven drivers point the same way

### Key Insights

- India's path to $6T by 2030 [[1]](#s01-ref-1) runs through firms that grow; the evidence ties stalled growth to owners who are stretched and cannot delegate without better information [[4]](#s01-ref-4), [[5]](#s01-ref-5).
- The status quo — the owner as integration layer — is being squeezed from four sides at once: cost, pressure, trust and law.
- AI cost is no longer the constraint: inference prices for a fixed capability fall about 40× a year [[8]](#s01-ref-8). The constraint is the owner's trust.
- Data protection law [[12]](#s01-ref-12) flips the risk calculation: informal information flows become the liability, and governed software becomes the safe choice.
- India-hosted frontier AI [[15]](#s01-ref-15) removes the last easy reason for an owner to say no.

### Investment Implications

The drivers favour a company that moves now. Costs are falling for everyone, so the advantage will not come from cheaper AI; it will come from being the first name owners associate with Owner Intelligence, and from the trust built while the law and the market shift. The window is roughly the DPDP phase-in period to May 2027: during that time owners are re-examining how information flows through their businesses, and the category leader will be the company in the room when they do.

# 02 — TAM/SAM/SOM: Sizing a Category That Has No Analyst Report Yet

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — SAM filters flagged LOW confidence (see "Confidence and gaps")

## Executive Summary

No research firm publishes the size of the **Owner Intelligence** market, because the category does not exist yet. Inventing a number would break the evidence standard this plan holds itself to, so Aroh's market is sized three independent ways and the three are checked against each other.

- **Bottom-up accounts (Layer 3):** about **38,000** Indian businesses have ₹100–₹5,000 Cr in revenue [[3]](#s02-ref-3). At an annual contract value of ₹18L, that is a **₹6,841 Cr** recurring revenue opportunity (TAM). Filtered to standalone, digitally mature businesses in the launch sectors, the base serviceable market (SAM) is about **8,400 accounts, or ₹1,505 Cr a year** (range ₹766–₹2,586 Cr).
- **Budgets already spent (Layer 2):** an owner's office already costs about **₹21.6–₹31.7L a year** in executive assistants, analysts and AI seats [[8]](#s02-ref-8), [[9]](#s02-ref-9), [[10]](#s02-ref-10), [[11]](#s02-ref-11), [[12]](#s02-ref-12). Aroh's ₹18L is 57–83% of what owners already pay for parts of this job.
- **Cost of the problem (Layer 1):** in a field experiment on Indian firms, better management raised profit by about **$230,000 a year per firm** (about ₹2.2 Cr) [[6]](#s02-ref-6). Aroh's fee is about 8% of that value at stake.

The plan's FY2029–30 target — 500 customers and ₹105.5 Cr of annual recurring revenue (Section 07) — is **6.0% of the base SAM**, above this plan's 5% red-flag line, and 3.5% of the broad SAM. The strategy document's 250-customer target [[16]](#s02-ref-16), now the plan's downside, would be 3.0%. The SAM itself rests on three filters that no public data yet supports, so this section flags them and proposes how to replace them with evidence.

## The category is sized three ways because none of the ways is enough alone

| Layer | Question it answers | Main source | Confidence |

## Layer 1 — The cost of the problem is growth the owner never pursues

The founding team's insight is that the real cost of the owner's ambient anxiety is not the hours spent chasing; it is the growth the business never pursues while its most valuable person watches operations. The research gives that cost a size.

- **What better management is worth.** In a randomised experiment on Indian textile firms, introducing better management practices raised productivity by 17% in the first year [[6]](#s02-ref-6). The working-paper version estimates the gain at about **$230,000 a year in profit per firm** — roughly **₹2.19 Cr** at ₹95/US$ [[6]](#s02-ref-6).
- **Why firms hadn't done it already.** The same study found that informational barriers were a primary reason, and that owners — already working **68 hours a week** — kept decisions within the family because they did not trust outsiders [[6]](#s02-ref-6).
- **How much of an owner's week is reactive.** CEOs of large companies spend about **36%** of their time reacting to unfolding issues [[7]](#s02-ref-7). Applied to a 68-hour owner week, that is roughly **24.5 hours a week** spent reacting — about a third of the owner's time. (The two studies cover very different populations; treat this as an illustration, not a measurement.)

| Measure | Value | Confidence |
| ------- | ----: | ---------- |
| Profit gain from better management, per firm per year | ₹2.19 Cr | MEDIUM (Indian textile RCT, 2008–2011) |
| Aroh ACV as % of that gain | 8.2% | Derived |
| Value at stake across the base SAM (8,361 accounts × ₹2.19 Cr) | ₹18,269 Cr/yr | LOW (transfer to other sectors and sizes) |
| Reactive owner hours per week | ~24.5 | LOW (mixed populations) |

**How to read Layer 1:** this is *value at stake*, not revenue Aroh can capture. Its job is to show that the price is small next to the problem. In Ogilvy's terms, the fact to put in front of an owner is simple: **₹18L a year to go after a ₹2 Cr problem.**

## Confidence and gaps: the SAM needs evidence before it carries weight

The plan's own rule is to stop and flag if more than 30% of the sizing rests on LOW-confidence inputs. **The SAM does**: all three filters are planning assumptions. The TAM (MEDIUM), the budget pool (MEDIUM) and the value at stake (MEDIUM for the per-firm figure) are sourced.

**Ways to replace the assumptions with evidence:**

1. **Design-partner discovery (recommended):** survey 50–100 promoters in the launch sectors on group structure, systems in use and willingness to pay. This doubles as the first sales pipeline.
2. **Buy the data:** a sector-by-band extract from an MCA data provider (Probe42 or CMIE Prowess) replaces the sector filter with HIGH-confidence counts.
3. **Model on TAM until validated:** use the 38,003 accounts with the penetration bands above, and present the SAM only as a range.

### Key Insights

- About **38,000** Indian businesses sit in the ₹100–₹5,000 Cr band [[3]](#s02-ref-3): a ₹6,841 Cr annual opportunity at ₹18L per account.
- Owners already spend **₹21.6–₹31.7L a year** on pieces of the chief-of-staff job [[8]](#s02-ref-8)–[[12]](#s02-ref-12); Aroh's price fits inside that.
- Better management was worth about **₹2.19 Cr a year per firm** in Indian field evidence [[6]](#s02-ref-6); Aroh costs about 8% of that.
- The base SAM of about 8,400 accounts makes the plan's 500 FY2029–30 customers **6.0%** penetration — a red flag that the owner has chosen to keep and validate; the 250-customer downside is 3.0%.

### Investment Implications

The Owner Intelligence category does not need heroic penetration to become a large business: even the downside's 250 customers is ₹45 Cr of exit ARR from a base of 38,000 eligible businesses. The base case's 500 asks more of the market, and the plan says so. The more important finding for an investor is that the price is small against both the money owners already spend and the value at stake. The weak point is the SAM filters, and the cheapest way to strengthen them — talking to 50–100 promoters — is also the first step of the go-to-market plan.

<a name="section-03"></a>

# 03 — Technology Architecture: The Product Is the Proof of the Point of View

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

A category is only as believable as the product that demonstrates it. Aroh's architecture is built to prove one claim every day: that **Owner Intelligence** can watch a whole business, tell the owner what matters, show its sources and act only with permission.

Aroh does not start from zero. Its building blocks run in production today as Metorite, developed by Hathi Labs: ten live apps, an approval card before any change, row-level separation of each company's data, a model router that can switch AI providers, and databases hosted in Mumbai [[3]](#s03-ref-3). What Aroh adds is the Chief of Staff layer on top — connectors to the systems owners already use, an exception engine, the 07:30 brief and 18:30 close-out, and an Enterprise Execution Graph that learns what actually leads to good and bad outcomes [[5]](#s03-ref-5).

Two architectural choices follow from the category's promise. First, **trust is designed in**, because software that can read email and change records has a far larger blast radius than a chatbot. Second, **AI processing moves into India before the first paid contract.** Aroh runs on DeepSeek V4-class models today at a measured ₹0.18 per request [[4]](#s03-ref-4); because those model weights are openly licensed [[7]](#s03-ref-7), the same class of model can be served from Indian infrastructure [[1]](#s03-ref-1), [[8]](#s03-ref-8) — so every paying customer's data is both stored and processed in India.

---

## Aroh works the way a good chief of staff works

A good chief of staff observes, filters, prepares and acts within a mandate. Aroh's autonomy model follows the same four steps, with defaults that keep the owner in charge [[5]](#s03-ref-5):

| Step | Example | Default |
| ---- | ------- | ------- |
| **Observe** | Detect that debtor ageing has worsened | Automatic |
| **Recommend** | Suggest a review with the CFO | Automatic |
| **Prepare** | Draft the email, WhatsApp reply or CRM update | Automatic |
| **Execute** | Send the message, change the record, approve the workflow | Set by policy; sensitive actions always need explicit approval |

Autonomy is configured by **user × system × action × amount**. A reminder to an internal sales manager and approval of a large payment never share a permission.

**A day with Aroh:**

| Time | What happens |
| ---- | ------------ |
| 07:30 | "Three things deserve your attention today" — e.g., receivables over a threshold have worsened, a key customer's forecast has changed, a factory milestone now threatens a commitment |
| 07:35 | The owner taps the first item. Aroh reconciles ERP receivables, CRM account data, the CFO's email and an authorised message thread. Every statement links to its source. |
| 07:37 | Aroh suggests three actions, marking which are recommended and which need approval |
| 07:38 | The owner approves a message to the regional head, a CRM follow-up and a CFO review; Aroh watches for the reply |
| During the day | Silence — unless a threshold is crossed, a commitment is missed or something changes a decision |
| 18:30 | "Seven actions started; five completed; two need attention tomorrow." |

## Production building blocks already exist as Metorite

Aroh is assembled from building blocks that are already running, not from a slide [[3]](#s03-ref-3):

| Building block | Status today | Role in Aroh |
| -------------- | ------------ | ------------ |
| 10 live apps — My Tasks, Calendar, Profile, Access, Projects, People, AI Chat, Approvals, Organisation, Appearance | Live | The owner's and executive team's workspace |
| Approval card before any AI change | Live | The "nothing happens without me" guarantee |
| Row-level multi-tenancy | Live | Each company's data kept separate in the database itself |
| Model router (LiteLLM) across providers | Live (DeepSeek in production; Claude, OpenAI, Gemini configured as options) | Vendor-neutral model plane |
| Specialist agents — tasks, CRM, projects, sales (Zoho CRM), delivery, triage, reconciliation, billing, strategy | Registered | Seed of the Chief of Staff's skills |
| Evaluation harness (17 golden trajectories) | Built | Regression tests for agent behaviour |
| Email (Microsoft 365, Gmail, IMAP), WhatsApp Business via Meta's official platform, CRM, meeting notes | Preview | The channels Aroh watches |
| Postgres + pgvector on Supabase, Mumbai region | Live | India-hosted system of record |

**Gaps, stated plainly** [[3]](#s03-ref-3): email and WhatsApp are still in preview; there is no durable job queue yet; long-term memory is off by default; several security controls are designed but not yet enforced; and only two organisations run in production. These are the first items on the build plan below.

## What Aroh adds turns a workspace into Owner Intelligence

| Layer | What it does | Why the category needs it |
| ----- | ------------ | ------------------------- |
| **Business connectors** | Reads ERP/accounting (e.g., Tally, Zoho, Odoo), CRM, email, calendar and authorised WhatsApp Business flows | The owner's context lives in many systems; Aroh must see across them |
| **Exception engine** | Thresholds and patterns that decide what deserves attention | "Silence unless something matters" is the product |
| **Brief and close-out** | 07:30 brief, 18:30 close-out, on mobile and WhatsApp | The daily promise customers can check |
| **Provenance** | "Why Aroh knows this" on every claim | Trust in the number |
| **Policy engine** | Permissions by user × system × action × amount | Act only within the mandate |
| **Enterprise Execution Graph** | Person → Account → Order → Project → Commitment → Decision → Action → Outcome | Organisational memory and the data moat |

**WhatsApp is the cockpit, not the database.** Aroh connects only through Meta's official WhatsApp Business Platform [[3]](#s03-ref-3). It will not scrape personal sessions. Reading existing group conversations depends on Meta's platform policy, so the plan treats it as a dependency, not a promised feature [[5]](#s03-ref-5).

## The moat is the Execution Graph, not the customer's data

Aroh will not build its value on owning customer data — that would make it harder to sell and harder to acquire [[5]](#s03-ref-5). The defensible asset is what Aroh learns about *how work gets done*:

1. **Organisational memory per customer** — which promises were made, who was assigned, what happened. This raises switching costs and improves relevance month by month.
2. **Product-level evaluation intelligence** — anonymised or permissioned failure cases, synthetic evaluations and policy templates that make Aroh better without exposing any one customer's content.
3. **Workflow intelligence** — which signals actually come before a missed collection, a lost sale or a late project. This is worth far more than raw chat history.

The progression: data aggregation → business context → execution graph → predictive exceptions → trusted actions → organisational memory.

## AI processing moves into India before the first paid contract

**Today.** Metorite routes requests to DeepSeek V4 models through DeepSeek's own API: V4-Pro for reasoning and V4-Flash for routine work [[3]](#s03-ref-3). Measured over 15 users, a main-model request costs **$0.0030** and a cheaper-model request **$0.0008**; with half of requests on the cheaper model, a request costs about **₹0.18** at ₹95/US$ [[4]](#s03-ref-4). Customer data is stored in Mumbai, but AI processing happens outside India.

**Why that has to change before Aroh takes money.** India's Finance Ministry told its officials to avoid AI tools such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek on office devices, citing the confidentiality of government data [[10]](#s03-ref-10). An owner's procurement team will raise the same question. An Owner Intelligence product that reads the owner's email, ERP and messages cannot ask for that trust while processing outside the country.

**How it moves.** DeepSeek released V4 as open weights under the MIT licence — V4-Pro with 1.6T total parameters (49B active per token) and V4-Flash with 284B (13B active) [[7]](#s03-ref-7). That means the same class of model can run on Indian infrastructure:

| Route | What exists today | Trade-off |
| ----- | ----------------- | --------- |
| Indian cloud hosts of open models | Yotta and Krutrim have served DeepSeek models from Indian data centres [[8]](#s03-ref-8), [[9]](#s03-ref-9) | Fastest; depends on a provider offering V4-class models at scale |
| Self-hosting on Indian GPUs | IndiaAI Compute lists 8 × H200 at ₹785/hour on a 12-month reservation [[1]](#s03-ref-1) | Full control; Aroh carries the operations work |
| Indian model providers | Sarvam serves its own models from Indian data centres (Section 04) | Different models; quality at Aroh's tasks to be tested |
| Frontier models in Indian regions | Claude Opus 5, Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5 on Amazon Bedrock in Mumbai and Hyderabad [[2]](#s03-ref-2) | Escalation path for the hardest reasoning; higher cost per request |

**Decision (owner, Stage 3):**

1. **Pilots** may run on the current API, with written disclosure that AI processing happens outside India.
2. **Every paid contract** runs on India-hosted processing, through one of the routes above, behind Aroh's own model router.
3. **The financial model** prices AI at today's measured DeepSeek V4 cost per request. This is an owner assumption — an India-hosted provider may charge a premium — and Stage 4 tests it in sensitivity analysis.

**When self-hosting pays.** The source strategy document estimated that private hosting would beat API pricing at around 35–40 organisations, using an assumed ₹0.40 per interaction [[5]](#s03-ref-5). At the measured ₹0.18, the bar is higher. A node large enough for V4-Pro (8 × H200, an engineering estimate) costs about ₹8.8L a month all-in:

| Usage level | API cost per org/month (measured) | Organisations needed for a self-hosted node to break even |
| ----------- | --------------------------------: | --------------------------------------------------------: |
| Typical user (0.1×) | ₹1,716 | Never (API cheaper than per-org variable cost) |
| Average active day (0.55×) | ₹9,435 | ~137 |
| Busiest user today (1.0×) | ₹17,155 | ~62 |

*Node = 8 × H200 at ₹785/hr × 730 hrs (₹5.73L) + ₹1.08L overhead + ₹2.0L MLOps = ₹8.81L/month fixed, plus ₹3,000 per org variable [[1]](#s03-ref-1), [[5]](#s03-ref-5). Break-even = fixed cost ÷ (API cost − variable cost) per org. How many organisations one node can serve at acceptable latency is unvalidated and must be load-tested. 10 users per org; 22 active days; 50% cheaper-model routing [[4]](#s03-ref-4).*

At the base-case ramp (150 customers at the end of FY2027–28, 300 a year later), self-hosting starts to make economic sense during FY2027–28 — and earlier if India-hosted API providers charge a large premium. The trigger is measured utilisation, not customer count. A dedicated private deployment for a single customer is available on request, priced to cover its cost, and is not part of the base financial model.

## Trust is designed in, because an agent with access has a larger blast radius

| Control | Purpose |
| ------- | ------- |
| Tenant isolation at the database row level | One company can never see another's data |
| Per-connector scopes and least-privilege credentials | Aroh reads only what the owner authorises |
| Approval workflows and deterministic action policies | Nothing sensitive happens without a human |
| Tamper-resistant action log | Every action can be audited |
| Provenance on every important claim | The owner can check where a number came from |
| Prompt-injection defences and data-loss prevention | Email and messages cannot instruct Aroh to act |
| Written employee consent before ingesting email or WhatsApp | Meets data-protection expectations from day one |
| Data stored in India; AI processing in India for every paid customer; customer-managed encryption for large deployments | Removes the procurement objection |

**Certification roadmap and cost** (mid-point estimates, not quotes) [[4]](#s03-ref-4):

| Item | Why it matters | Estimate |
| ---- | -------------- | -------: |
| Google restricted-scope security review (Gmail) | Required before Gmail access goes live | ₹2.2L |
| External penetration test | Independent proof the controls hold | ₹3.0L |
| ISO 27001 certificate | Asked for by larger organisations | ₹5.0L |
| DPDP compliance work | Policies, contracts and processes | ₹2.5L |
| **Total one-time** | | **₹12.7L** |

## Perception fixes deliver felt value for less than engineering fixes

Rory Sutherland's rule: before paying to change reality, ask whether changing perception delivers the same felt value [[6]](#s03-ref-6). For each worry an owner has, Aroh's roadmap chooses the cheaper fix first:

| What the owner feels | Expensive engineering fix | Cheaper perception fix Aroh ships first |
| -------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| "Is it still watching?" | Real-time streaming of every event | A midday "nothing needs you" check-in |
| "Can I trust this number?" | Perfect accuracy everywhere | Source shown and a confidence label on every claim |
| "Is it doing things behind my back?" | Fine-grained autonomy settings at launch | An approval card before action, and the 18:30 log of everything done |
| "Why is it slow?" | Faster, costlier models | A progress line: "Checking Tally and your inbox…" |
| "Setup will take months" | Every integration on day one | A useful first brief in week one from email plus one core system |

## Aroh keeps what a human chief of staff provides

The doorman fallacy warns against replacing a role by its visible task and losing its unseen value [[6]](#s03-ref-6). A human chief of staff gives more than a schedule:

| Unseen value of a human chief of staff | How Aroh keeps it |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Discretion | Permissions per person and topic; sensitive items go only to the owner |
| Judgment | Ranked priorities with reasons; learns from what the owner accepts or overrides |
| Loyalty | Works for the owner; customer data stays the customer's |
| Memory | The Execution Graph remembers every commitment and outcome |
| Tact | Drafts in the owner's voice; nothing sent without approval |

## Swarms of agents build, run and support the platform

The founders' plan is to run Aroh the way it expects its customers to run: with AI agents doing a large share of the engineering, DevOps and customer-support work, supervised by a small human team. That needs real compute, so the financial model budgets it explicitly rather than treating it as a rounding error:

| Layer | What it pays for | Budget (base case) |
| ----- | ---------------- | ------------------ |
| **Development and operations cloud** | Development and test environments, continuous integration, staging, and always-on agent swarms for engineering, DevOps and support | ₹0.4 Cr in pre-launch, then ₹2.0 / 4.0 / 7.0 Cr a year (half that in the downside until customers justify it) |
| **Production infrastructure** | India-hosted compute, RAM, storage, bandwidth and internet connectivity, backups and disaster recovery, monitoring and security tooling | ₹4L a month platform floor plus ₹3,000 per customer a month |
| **AI processing** | Model calls for customers' briefs, drafts and actions | About ₹944 per user per month at average usage (measured) |
| **People's equipment and tools** | Laptops for every hire (₹2.3L engineers, ₹1.2L others) and Claude plans for developers and engineers | In the cost model (Section 08) |

The bet is that agents raise each person's output enough to keep headcount near 53 at 500 customers. That is a founder-stated assumption, not an observed result; the first evidence will be how many engineer-hours each new customer takes (target: 20) and how much support is resolved without a human.

## The build plan turns building blocks into Aroh within the 3-year horizon

| Phase | Focus | Exit test |
| ----- | ----- | --------- |
| **Year 1 — Prove it** | Close the production gaps (job queue, enforced security, email and WhatsApp out of preview); ship connectors for the launch sectors' common systems; 07:30 brief and 18:30 close-out; India-hosted AI processing before the first paid contract; Google security review and penetration test | Design partners use the brief daily; approvals work end to end |

| **Year 3 — Scale it** | Dedicated deployments on request; private serving where measured utilisation justifies it; deeper autonomy for proven workflows | Gross margin holds as customers grow |

The build plan's discipline: **every custom connector or bespoke dashboard pushes Aroh towards services economics; every reusable capability pushes it towards software economics** [[5]](#s03-ref-5).

### Key Insights

- Aroh's building blocks already run in production as Metorite [[3]](#s03-ref-3); the gaps are known and listed.
- Aroh runs on DeepSeek V4-class models at a measured ₹0.18 per request [[4]](#s03-ref-4); because the weights are openly licensed [[7]](#s03-ref-7), processing moves to Indian infrastructure before the first paid contract.

- Perception fixes (sources shown, check-ins, approval cards) deliver the "control that brings calm" feeling before expensive engineering does.
- The Execution Graph, not customer data, is the moat.

### Investment Implications

Investors are not funding a research project. The core platform exists, runs in production and has a measured cost per user. Capital goes into the layer that makes it a category — connectors, the exception engine, the Execution Graph and the certifications that let owners say yes. The API-first decision keeps fixed infrastructure cost low until usage proves otherwise, protecting runway while the category is being established.

<a name="section-04"></a>

# 04 — Competitive Analysis: Aroh Competes With Categories, Not Products

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-24
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

Aroh's competition is not a list of products with feature checkboxes. It is seven existing categories of software, each built for someone other than the owner: per-employee copilots, agents inside systems of record, personal AI assistants, agent-building platforms, Indian AI providers, business intelligence dashboards and a crowd of small tools calling themselves an "AI chief of staff" [[26]](#s04-ref-26). Each serves a real buyer well. None is built to watch the whole business on the owner's behalf — which is the gap **Owner Intelligence** names.

The most serious pressure comes from two directions. Frontier AI labs are shipping proactive personal agents that brief, draft and ask for approval — Anthropic's Claude Duet and OpenAI's dots both launched in the last two months [[9]](#s04-ref-9), [[10]](#s04-ref-10). And Zoho, the Indian-built business suite, now runs its own AI model in Indian data centres [[6]](#s04-ref-6). Aroh's answer to both is the same: it works for the owner, across every system the business uses — including Tally, WhatsApp Business and whichever ERP or CRM is already in place — hosted in India and priced per organisation.

Strategic buyers have already paid for this shape of asset elsewhere: ServiceNow completed its $2.4B acquisition of Moveworks in December 2025 [[23]](#s04-ref-23), and Workday agreed to buy Sana for about $1.1B [[24]](#s04-ref-24). The category is valued; the Indian owner is unserved.

## Owner Intelligence is designed to be easier to adopt than a CRM or an ERP

The founders' view is that this product will spread faster than the systems it sits above. A CRM or ERP asks a company to change how its people work: data entry, training, migration, months of rollout. Owner Intelligence asks the owner to read a brief and approve or decline a few actions, on top of systems the company already runs. Fewer people have to change, the first value shows up in days (a brief at 07:30) rather than months, and the first user — the owner — is the person who decides.

If that is right, adoption should travel by word of mouth among promoters and founders, who talk to each other and trust each other's recommendations far more than they trust vendors. This is a **founder-stated assumption, not yet evidence**: the first ten pilots and fifty contracts (Section 05) will show whether time-to-first-value and referral rates are what the founders expect, and the plan's penetration targets (Section 02) rest on it.

## Seven moats, built through the first 500 customers

None of these exists at scale yet. Each is earned through deployments:

1. **Category ownership.** The name Owner Intelligence and the point of view, claimed first in India.
2. **The Enterprise Execution Graph.** Organisational memory of commitments, decisions and outcomes that deepens every month a customer stays.
3. **Indian connectors.** Tally, Indian ERPs, WhatsApp Business through Meta's official platform — integrations global players have little reason to build.
4. **Trust credentials.** India hosting, approvals before action, audit logs, ISO 27001 and data-protection compliance (Section 03).
5. **The owner's daily habit.** The 07:30 brief becomes the first thing the owner reads; habits are hard to displace.
6. **Onboarding playbook.** A repeatable 40-hour setup that competitors must learn customer by customer.
7. **Outcome data.** Knowledge of which signals actually come before a missed collection or a late project — learned only by watching many businesses.

### Key Insights

- Aroh competes with seven categories built for other buyers; none is built for the owner across the whole business [[26]](#s04-ref-26).
- The closest new threat is frontier labs' personal agents (Claude Duet, OpenAI dots) [[9]](#s04-ref-9), [[10]](#s04-ref-10) — personal, US-first and inbox-centred; Aroh is organisational, India-hosted and ERP-aware.
- The "AI chief of staff" label is crowded but owned by no one, and no product using it is based in India [[26]](#s04-ref-26) — the reason Aroh claims Owner Intelligence instead.
- Strategic buyers have paid $1.1B–$2.4B for cross-system AI front doors [[23]](#s04-ref-23), [[24]](#s04-ref-24).

### Investment Implications

The competitive map shows a well-funded field moving towards Aroh's idea from the edges — labs from the personal side, suites from the employee side, platforms from the developer side — while nobody starts from the Indian owner. That leaves a window measured in quarters, not years. Aroh's strategy is to use it to claim the category name, earn trust with the first customers, and build the India-specific connectors and organisational memory that a global player would rather buy than build.

# 05 — Customer Validation: What Is Proven, What Is Not, and How the Pilots Will Tell Us

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

Aroh has no paying customers today, and this section says so first. What it has is the raw material for evidence: a working product used daily inside one company, measured usage from 15 people, and ten friendly companies — earning ₹10–300 Cr a year — willing to try it [[1]](#s05-ref-1), [[2]](#s05-ref-2).

The category's central claim — that owners already feel the problem of being the integration layer — is supported by outside evidence rather than by Aroh's own customers. Indian owners in a field study worked 68-hour weeks and held decisions close because they lacked information to delegate [[5]](#s05-ref-5); two-thirds of Indian CEOs worry about keeping pace with technology and AI [[6]](#s05-ref-6). Aroh's job in its first year is to turn that outside evidence into its own: owners who open the 07:30 brief every day, act on it, and pay for it.

The plan for doing that is specific and dated. **Ten free pilots finish by December 2026; fifty more owners sign paid contracts by March 2027** — about 57 paying customers before the first full financial year [[3]](#s05-ref-3). Each pilot measures the same handful of numbers, and a discovery programme with 50–100 promoters replaces the low-confidence filters in the market sizing with data.

## Owners already feel the problem — the evidence is outside Aroh today

Category design asks whether customers recognise the problem before they see the product. The outside evidence says Indian owners do:

- **Owners are stretched and can't delegate.** In a randomised study of Indian firms, owners averaged 68 hours a week; decisions stayed within the family because owners did not trust outsiders, and lack of information was a primary reason better practices were not adopted [[5]](#s05-ref-5).
- **CEOs name the anxiety.** 66% of Indian CEOs are concerned about keeping pace with technology and AI, against 42% of CEOs globally naming it their top concern [[6]](#s05-ref-6).
- **Owners already pay for partial fixes.** An owner's office typically carries ₹21.6–₹31.7L a year in executive assistants, MIS staff and analysts (Section 02).

What Aroh does not yet have is **behaviour from its own buyers** — Rory Sutherland's distinction between what people say and what they do [[7]](#s05-ref-7). The pilots are designed to capture behaviour.

## The pipeline: ten friendly companies, with a caveat

| Pipeline | Detail | Source |
| -------- | ------ | ------ |
| Friendly companies | 10, through the founders' and DEWiN's networks | Founder input [[4]](#s05-ref-4) |
| Annual revenue range | ₹10–300 Cr | Founder input [[4]](#s05-ref-4) |
| Fit with the buyer profile (₹100–5,000 Cr) | Partial: the ₹10–100 Cr firms sit below the profile | Section 02 |
| Pre-launch plan | 10 free pilots by Dec 2026 (from this pipeline); 50 further paid contracts by Mar 2027 from the founders' and DEWiN's wider networks | Owner decision [[3]](#s05-ref-3) |

**The caveat.** At ₹18L a year, Aroh would cost a ₹10 Cr company 1.8% of its revenue. The smaller friendly firms are valuable as pilots, references and product feedback, but the plan does not count on them as paying customers. Paying pilots are qualified to the ₹100 Cr+ profile.

## The fifty contracts rest on relationships DEWiN has already built

The base case assumes fifty owners sign paid contracts between January and March 2027. The founders' confidence rests on two things:

1. **Existing relationships.** DEWiN has already built relationships with owners and leaders in its network and will use them to bring owners into the first pilots and contracts.
2. **Ease of adoption and word of mouth.** The founders expect Owner Intelligence to be adopted more easily and more widely than a CRM or ERP, with owners recommending it to peers in leadership and founder circles (Section 04).

Both are **founder-stated and unproven** until contracts exist. To turn them into evidence before the pre-launch period ends, the plan adds a **named-prospect list**: every owner expected to sign, with the relationship owner, the date of the last conversation, the loop they would start with and a stated probability. The list is reviewed monthly from November 2026 and shared with any investor before the seed.

## Every pilot measures the same five numbers

A six-week pilot runs on one business loop — receivables, key accounts, order exceptions or project milestones — chosen with the owner [[3]](#s05-ref-3).

| Measure | What "working" looks like | Why it matters |
| ------- | ------------------------- | -------------- |
| **Daily brief opens** | Owner opens the 07:30 brief on most working days | The habit is the product (Section 04) |
| **Items acted on** | Owner approves or delegates at least one item most days | Proof the brief surfaces what matters |
| **Engineer-hours to go live** | ≤ 20 | Onboarding economics (Section 08) |
| **AI requests per user per day** | Within the measured range (0.1×–1× of 432) | Cost to serve (Section 07) |
| **Conversion to a paid contract** | ₹6L setup + ₹18L annual | Willingness to pay |

At the end of each pilot the owner is also asked one Sutherland-style question about feeling, not features: *"Do you worry less about what you might be missing?"* — the psychological job Owner Intelligence exists to do (Section 00).

## Discovery with 50–100 promoters replaces assumptions with data

The market sizing flagged three filters with no public data: how many businesses are standalone rather than group subsidiaries, how many are in the launch sectors, and how many run systems Aroh can watch (Section 02). A structured discovery programme answers all three while building the sales pipeline:

| Question | Replaces |
| -------- | -------- |

| What does the owner's office cost today? | Budget-pool estimate (Layer 2) |
| Would you pay ₹1.5L a month for the 07:30 brief and follow-through? | Pricing confidence |

The existing owners fund that period themselves (Section 09), so outside investors are asked to price the company only after the evidence is in — not to believe in traction that does not yet exist.

# 06 — Go-To-Market: Sell the Problem First, Then Make Saying Yes Feel Safe

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — channel mix and budget split are proposals pending Stage 5 sign-off

## Executive Summary

A new category cannot be sold the way an existing product is sold. Owners do not search for "Owner Intelligence"; they lie awake wondering what they are missing. Aroh's go-to-market therefore **sells the problem before the product** — the owner's head as the integration layer — and then makes the decision to try Aroh feel as safe as doing nothing [[1]](#s06-ref-1), [[2]](#s06-ref-2).

The motion is the same at every stage: a conversation about what the owner might be missing, a free six-week pilot on one business loop, and a contract once the 07:30 brief has proven itself — ₹6L to set up and ₹18L a year [[4]](#s06-ref-4). Founders sell in the pre-launch period; a sales lead and account executives join from FY2027–28; the marketing budget rises from ₹20L to about ₹9.6 Cr — at least 10% of revenue from FY2028–29 — to make Owner Intelligence a recognised category before global players arrive [[4]](#s06-ref-4).

The plan starts with a sprint: **ten free pilots by December 2026 and fifty owners signing paid contracts directly by March 2027** on the strength of those pilots and the founders' networks. From FY2027–28 it needs about **141, 236 and 329 free pilots** a year to reach 500 customers [[5]](#s06-ref-5). Those are the numbers every channel and every rupee of marketing is measured against.

---

## The motion: diagnose, pilot, prove, contract

| Step | What happens | Who | Signal it sends (Sutherland) |
| ---- | ------------ | --- | ---------------------------- |
| **1. "What might you be missing?"** | A 60-minute conversation with the owner about the decisions they worry about and the systems they check by hand | Founder / account executive | We understand your problem before we pitch anything |
| **2. Free pilot, six weeks** | One business loop (receivables, key accounts, orders, projects); the 07:30 brief and 18:30 close-out start in week one. In pre-launch, owners who have seen the first ten pilots may sign directly | Forward-deployed engineer, on site | We pay to prove it — the costly signal is ours, not yours |
| **3. Proof review** | Owner sees brief opens, actions taken and what Aroh caught | Founder + FDE | Evidence, not adjectives (Ogilvy) |
| **4. Contract** | ₹6L setup + ₹18L a year, billed annually; no discounts | Founder / account executive | The price says "this runs your business" |
| **5. Expand** | More loops, more connectors, extra executive seats (₹10,000 each a month) | Customer success | Value grows with use |

## Making yes feel safe removes the owner's risk of being blamed

Sutherland's observation is that buyers fear being blamed for a bad decision more than they value a good one [[2]](#s06-ref-2). Doing nothing — checking everything personally — is the decision nobody is ever blamed for. Aroh's go-to-market removes the reasons a promoter could be blamed for trying it:

| What an owner (or their CFO) might worry about | What Aroh puts in place |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| "Our data leaves the country" | Data stored in India; AI processing in India for every paid customer (Section 03) |
| "It will act without asking" | An approval card before any sensitive action; a log of everything done |
| "We'll pay and it won't work" | Free pilot on one loop; pay only after the proof review |
| "Nobody we know uses it" | Pilots from the founders' network become named references |
| "It's a Chinese AI model" | Pilots disclose where processing happens; paid contracts run on India-hosted models (Section 03) |

## Channels phase in as the category becomes known

| Period | Primary channel | Supporting channels (approved) | Onboardings needed |
| ------ | --------------- | ------------------------------ | -----------------: |
| Pre-launch (Nov 2026–Mar 2027) | Founders and DEWiN network; the 10 friendly companies | References from the first pilots | 10 pilots + 50 direct |
| FY2027–28 | Sales lead + 4 account executives, with founders | Promoter referrals; owner roundtables | 141 pilots |
| FY2028–29 | Sales lead + 7 account executives | Referral relationships with ERP / Tally implementation partners and chartered-accountant firms that advise promoters | 236 pilots |
| FY2029–30 | Sales lead + 10 account executives | Industry-association events in industrial cities; partner referrals | 329 pilots |

Pilot counts come from the revenue model at 70% conversion [[5]](#s06-ref-5). The supporting channels and any referral terms are proposals; Section 07's no-discount rule means referral rewards, if any, come from the sales budget rather than from the customer's price.

## Word of mouth among owners is the main growth engine

The go-to-market leans on how promoters actually decide: they listen to other promoters. DEWiN's relationships open the first doors; the founders' view is that the product's low adoption friction — a brief to read rather than a system to learn — makes each happy owner more likely to recommend it. The plan therefore treats referral as a measured channel: every pilot ends with the question "Who else should see this?", and the percentage of new customers who arrive by referral is tracked monthly alongside conversion and onboarding hours. This is an assumption the first fifty contracts will test.

## Lightning strikes: one concentrated launch moment a year

*Play Bigger* argues that a category is claimed through a few concentrated "lightning strikes" rather than a steady trickle of marketing [[1]](#s06-ref-1). Aroh plans one a year, each tied to a milestone:

| When | Lightning strike (proposed) | Milestone it rides on |
| ---- | --------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Start of FY2027–28 | Publish the Owner Intelligence point of view; launch with stories from the first ten pilots and fifty paying owners | India-hosted AI live; ~57 paying customers |
| FY2028–29 | First annual **Owner Intelligence Report** — anonymised, aggregated findings on what owners miss and how fast they find out | 150+ paying customers |
| FY2029–30 | Owner roadshows in industrial cities with customer promoters on stage | 300+ customers; referenceable sectors |

**Ogilvy's discipline for every message:** lead with the promise the owner can check — *"Wake up knowing what matters, why it matters, who needs to act — and what Aroh has already done about it"* — and support it with specific facts: the 07:30 brief, the approval card, data stored in India [[3]](#s06-ref-3).

### Key Insights

- The go-to-market sells the problem — the owner as integration layer — before the product, as category design prescribes [[1]](#s06-ref-1).
- The motion de-risks the decision for the owner: free pilot, approval before action, India-hosted processing, named references [[2]](#s06-ref-2).
- Ten pilots and fifty direct contracts by March 2027, then 141 / 236 / 329 pilots a year, reach 500 customers; every channel is measured against that [[5]](#s06-ref-5).
- One lightning strike a year, each tied to a milestone, turns marketing spend into category ownership.

### Investment Implications

Aroh's go-to-market is designed for a buyer who has never heard of the category and cannot afford to be wrong. The costs of proving value — the pilot, the engineer on site, the India hosting — fall on Aroh, not the owner, which is why conversion can be high once the brief is running. The decisive test comes first: fifty owners signing in the three months after the first pilots. After that the risk is volume — going from 141 pilots a year to 329 requires the sales team, referral channels and category awareness to arrive on schedule. The stress tests in Section 10 show the business stays solvent on DEWiN's ₹5 Cr bridge even if the ramp halves, as long as the pre-launch customers sign; hiring and marketing step-ups are tied to customer milestones either way.

<a name="section-07"></a>

# 07 — Revenue Model: One Price That Says "This Runs Your Business"

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — generated from the revenue model by `write_section07.py`

## Executive Summary

Aroh sells **Owner Intelligence** to the owner, not seats to employees, and its price is built to say so. There is one tier — **Command, at ₹1.5L per organisation per month** — billed annually upfront at ₹18L, with a ₹6L setup fee at signing [[1]](#s07-ref-1). Ten executive users are included; extra executive seats cost ₹10,000 a month, and contracts rise 7% a year at renewal.

The plan starts fast. Ten free pilots run by December 2026, and fifty more owners sign paid contracts by March 2027 — about **57 paying customers before the first full financial year** [[1]](#s07-ref-1). From there the base case grows to 150, 300 and 500 customers at the end of FY2027–28, FY2028–29 and FY2029–30, earning **₹25.38 Cr, ₹52.59 Cr and ₹95.76 Cr** of revenue and leaving FY2029–30 with **₹106.3 Cr of annual recurring revenue** [[2]](#s07-ref-2). Existing customers pay more each year: net revenue retention is about **109%** after 10% annual churn.

The economics hold at the extremes, not only on average. A customer's AI and production-infrastructure cost is about ₹12,400 a month at average usage — **92% gross margin before people costs** — and stays at a quarter of the price even if every user works twice as hard as today's busiest user [[3]](#s07-ref-3).

---

## The price is a signal before it is a number

Rory Sutherland's argument is that price tells the buyer what kind of thing they are buying [[7]](#s07-ref-7). A per-seat price around the cost of a phone plan says "tool". An organisation-level price says "this runs your business".

| Signal | How the price carries it |
| ------ | ------------------------ |
| **This is for the owner** | Priced per organisation, not per employee; one tier, everything included |
| **This is serious** | ₹1.5L a month and a ₹6L setup fee — the same order as the people it works alongside |
| **We are confident** | One price, no discount ladder; the free pilot is the single concession |

**What Aroh is compared against matters.** Priced against software, ₹1.5L a month looks expensive: Microsoft 365 Copilot for ten executives costs about ₹25,000 a month [[8]](#s07-ref-8). Priced against the owner's office, it looks modest:

| What the owner pays for today | Per month |
| ----------------------------- | --------: |
| Microsoft 365 Copilot, 10 executives (₹2,495 × 10) [[8]](#s07-ref-8) | ₹24,950 |
| **Aroh Command** | **₹1,50,000** |
| Owner's office: executive assistant + MIS executive + business analyst + Copilot seats [[9]](#s07-ref-9) | ₹1,80,000 |
| Owner's office with a chief of staff instead of an analyst [[9]](#s07-ref-9) | ₹2,64,000 |
| Value at stake: profit gain from better management, per firm [[10]](#s07-ref-10) | ₹18,20,000 |

Aroh costs less than the office already built around the owner — and works across all of it — while going after a problem worth about twelve times its price.

## One tier, everything included: Command

| Element | Terms |
| ------- | ----- |
| **Subscription** | ₹1.5L per organisation per month, billed annually upfront (₹18L/year) |
| **Included** | 10 executive users; all connectors and features; 07:30 brief and 18:30 close-out; approvals; India-hosted processing |
| **Extra executive seats** | ₹10,000 per seat per month |
| **Setup fee** | ₹6L, invoiced at signing and recognised over the first contract year (onboarding takes about 20 engineer-hours) |
| **Free pilot** | Six weeks on one business loop — the route in from FY2027–28 |
| **Renewal** | Annual; contract value rises 7% at each renewal |
| **Billing** | Annual invoice, paid by bank transfer |
| **Pricing rule** | No discounts on the list price — early customers included |

**Why annual upfront.** It strengthens cash and avoids RBI's ₹15,000 limit on automatic recurring debits without extra authentication, which a ₹1.5L monthly charge would exceed [[4]](#s07-ref-4).

## Revenue grows three ways: new customers, uplift and seats

**Value of one customer by contract year** [[2]](#s07-ref-2): ₹18.0L in year one, ₹21.8L in year two (with two extra seats) and ₹26.1L in year three (four extra seats). Net revenue retention is 109% from year one to two and 108% from year two to three.

## The base case reaches ₹106.3 Cr of ARR by March 2030

**How the pre-launch period is shown.** It is a five-month stub (November 2026–March 2027), not a financial year, and it is not annualised. The ten free pilots end by December; about 7 convert and are live for roughly two months by 31 March. The fifty direct customers are onboarded during March, so each is live for only about half a month. Contracts are billed annually upfront, so the cash arrives in March while revenue is recognised over the following twelve months — subscription month by month, and the ₹6L setup fee spread over the first contract year. That is why pre-launch **recognised revenue is only ₹0.78 Cr while ₹13.68 Cr is billed**; the difference sits in deferred revenue and flows into FY2027–28. Each customer's first contract year runs from its signing date, so most pre-launch customers renew near the end of FY2027–28. Later customers sign evenly through each year [[2]](#s07-ref-2).

## The economics hold at the extremes, not only on average

Sutherland's warning — don't design for the average — applies to AI costs, which depend on how hard people use the product [[7]](#s07-ref-7). Variable cost per organisation (10 users: AI calls plus production infrastructure) at measured rates [[3]](#s07-ref-3):

| Usage | AI + production infrastructure per org/month | Share of ₹1.5L price | Gross margin before people costs |
| ----- | -------------------------: | -------------------: | -------------------------------: |
| Typical user (0.1×) | ₹4,700 | 3% | 97% |
| Average active day (0.55×) | ₹12,400 | 8% | **92%** |
| Busiest user today (1.0×) | ₹20,200 | 13% | 87% |
| Twice the busiest user (2.0×) | ₹37,300 | 25% | 75% |

The figures include ₹3,000 a month per customer of production infrastructure — about 3.4 times the ₹880 measured today — — compute, RAM, storage, bandwidth, backups and monitoring — on top of AI calls. Extra seats add AI cost only (about ₹1,000 a month against a ₹10,000 price, 90%). People costs, development infrastructure, customer acquisition cost and lifetime value are in Section 08.

### Key Insights

- One tier, ₹1.5L per organisation per month, signals an executive product; it is anchored against the owner's office (₹1.8–₹2.6L a month), not against per-seat software [[8]](#s07-ref-8), [[9]](#s07-ref-9).

- Net revenue retention of ~109% comes from a 7% uplift and extra seats, despite 10% churn.
- Variable gross margin is 92% at average usage and still 77% at twice the busiest user's usage [[3]](#s07-ref-3).

### Investment Implications

The revenue model makes Aroh an organisation-level software business with expanding customers, not a seat reseller with thin margins. Each customer is worth more every year it stays, and AI costs are a small and falling share of the price. The variable that decides everything is the first one: whether fifty owners sign by March 2027. The plan is built so that this is proven before outside investors are asked to price the company.

# 08 — Team & Costs: A Company Designed to Earn Trust, Teach the Category and Productise

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — generated from the cost model by `write_section08.py`

## Executive Summary

Category design says the company itself must be built to serve the category, not just the product [[1]](#s08-ref-1) — here, the Owner Intelligence category. Aroh's organisation does three jobs: **earn the owner's trust** in person (forward-deployed engineers on site), **teach the market a new category** (a marketing budget that grows with revenue), and **productise** what it learns so that onboarding stays near 20 engineer-hours per customer (developers building reusable connectors).

In the base case the team grows from **13 people at the end of the pre-launch period to 21, 33 and 53** at the end of FY2027–28, FY2028–29 and FY2029–30 [[3]](#s08-ref-3). The five-month pre-launch stub loses **₹2.70 Cr at EBITDA level** — the team is in place, but the fifty direct customers are onboarded only in March and contribute almost no recognised revenue yet. From FY2027–28 EBITDA is **₹9.93 Cr, ₹20.35 Cr and ₹41.67 Cr** [[3]](#s08-ref-3). If those fifty do not sign, the downside (the previous plan) loses ₹2.54 Cr, ₹5.65 Cr and ₹4.93 Cr before turning profitable — the case Section 09 funds against.

The unit economics are strong if the assumptions hold: each customer costs about **₹8.6L to acquire** in FY2029–30, pays that back in about **2.4 months** once the ₹6L setup fee is counted, and is worth about **₹94L over five years — 10.9 times its acquisition cost** [[3]](#s08-ref-3).

## The organisation is shaped by the category, not by a template

| Job the category demands | Who does it | Why this shape |
| ------------------------ | ----------- | -------------- |
| **Earn the owner's trust** | Forward-deployed engineers (FDEs), on site for pilots and onboarding | An owner hands over email, ERP and WhatsApp only to people they have met. Sutherland's costly signalling: turning up in person says "we are serious" in a way no demo can [[2]](#s08-ref-2). |
| **Teach the market a new category** | Founders, then a sales lead and account executives; marketing of at least 10% of revenue from FY2028–29 | Owners must recognise the problem before they buy the answer; that takes evangelism, events and stories. |
| **Productise what we learn** | Developers (4 → 4 → 6 → 10) | Every connector and exception pattern built once keeps onboarding near 20 hours — the difference between software and services economics [[4]](#s08-ref-4). |
| **Keep customers** | Customer success, one person per 30 live organisations | A new category needs more hand-holding than a mature one. |

# 09 — Fundraising: Fund the Pre-Launch, Then Raise on Proof

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — generated from the model by `write_section09.py`

## Executive Summary

Aroh's funding plan follows its sales plan. **DEWiN's existing shareholders put in ₹5 Cr now** to bridge the five-month pre-launch period — about ₹3.72 Cr of costs fall due before the March 2027 customer payments arrive — without bringing in outside investors [[1]](#s09-ref-1). If fifty owners sign paid contracts by March 2027, Aroh bills about **₹13.68 Cr upfront** and reaches about **₹10.3 Cr of annual recurring revenue (~$1.1M) before its first full financial year**; from then on customer prepayments fund the business and cash never falls below ₹15.0 Cr [[2]](#s09-ref-2).

The seed round is therefore raised **after** the milestone, on evidence, at a valuation set by paying customers — or not at all if cash flow covers growth. This reverses the usual order deliberately. Before any customer exists, the only Indian valuation range found for a seed round puts ₹48 Cr pre-money in its upper half [[3]](#s09-ref-3); with ₹10.3 Cr of ARR, even the median public-software multiple of 4.4× implies about ₹45 Cr [[4]](#s09-ref-4) — before any credit for growth.

The risk the plan funds against is that the fifty contracts do not arrive. Then the business follows the previous plan, which needs about **₹6.0 Cr** before it turns self-funding [[2]](#s09-ref-2). The answer is a seed of about ₹5 Cr raised at the start of FY2027–28 — below the median Indian seed round of ₹8.0 Cr [[5]](#s09-ref-5) — together with holding the development and operations cloud to half its base budget until customers justify it. That keeps cash above ₹4.0 Cr in the downside.

# 10 — Financial Projections: Owner Intelligence That Pays for Itself From the First Quarter

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft — generated from the financial model by `write_section10.py`

## Executive Summary

Aroh's base case is built on one early milestone: ten free pilots by December 2026 and fifty paid contracts by March 2027. If that happens, the five-month pre-launch stub is a **loss of ₹2.70 Cr** — recognised revenue is only ₹0.78 Cr because the fifty customers are onboarded in March and ₹13.68 Cr of annual contracts is billed upfront but recognised over the following year. The business is profitable from FY2027–28 and reaches **₹95.76 Cr of revenue, ₹106.3 Cr of annual recurring revenue and ₹41.67 Cr of EBITDA in FY2029–30**, with 500 customers [[1]](#s10-ref-1). DEWiN's ₹5 Cr bridges the pre-launch until the March payments arrive; after that, customer prepayments fund the business and cash ends FY2029–30 at ₹121.28 Cr, ₹65.19 Cr of which is customers' prepaid, not-yet-earned revenue.

Every figure below comes from the 14-sheet financial model, generated from the assumption register and checked against independent calculation scripts: revenue, gross profit and EBITDA agree within 0.5% in every period, the balance sheet balances to the rupee and cash never goes negative — in both the base and downside models [[1]](#s10-ref-1), [[2]](#s10-ref-2).

The plan's real risk is the milestone itself. Stress tests show that price, AI cost, churn, conversion and even a halved ramp are absorbed by the ₹5 Cr bridge — the constraint is the timing of the March receipts. Only the downside — no paid pre-launch customers and the slower previous plan (0 → 20 → 90 → 250) — needs outside money: about ₹6.0 Cr in total, which a ~₹5 Cr seed plus a gated agent-cloud budget covers (Section 09) [[3]](#s10-ref-3).

# 11 — Risk Analysis: The Decisive Risk Is That the Category Forms Slowly — or Around Someone Else

> **Last Updated:** 2026-10-25
> **Project:** Aroh
> **Status:** Draft

## Executive Summary

Most of Aroh's risks are ordinary for an early software company: an unproven price, an unfinished product, a small team. The risk that matters most is particular to category creation: **owners may adopt Owner Intelligence more slowly than the plan needs, or a better-funded company may claim the category first.** The plan concentrates the first risk into one dated milestone — **fifty paid contracts by March 2027** — and the stress tests show why it matters: with those customers, DEWiN's ₹5 Cr bridges the pre-launch (about ₹3.7 Cr of costs fall due before the March receipts) and customer prepayments then carry the business even if the later ramp halves; without them, the previous plan needs about ₹6.0 Cr of outside money [[1]](#s11-ref-1). The competitive map makes the second risk visible, with frontier AI labs now shipping proactive personal agents [[4]](#s11-ref-4).

Two risks are specific to how Aroh is built. Its AI runs today on DeepSeek's API outside India, while Indian government guidance has steered officials away from DeepSeek on office devices [[6]](#s11-ref-6); and its email and WhatsApp channels depend on Google's and Meta's platform reviews. Both have mitigations already in the plan: AI processing moves into India before the first paid contract, and both platform reviews are scheduled and budgeted in the pre-launch period.

Each risk below has an owner-level mitigation and an early-warning signal the team will track monthly.

| **One organisation-level price, ₹1.5L a month** | Self-serve ₹500 per seat plus AI credits (Metorite's original pricing) | At ₹500 a seat, about 4,760 paid seats are needed just to cover the founding team; the price would also signal "tool", not "runs your business" (Section 07) | No — but smaller friendly firms may be offered a seat-based trial outside the plan |
| **Free pilot, then full price** | Paid pilot (₹1.5–2L, credited to the contract) | Owner decision: lower friction for a new category; the ₹6L setup fee restores the commitment signal | **Yes** — if conversion falls below 50%, test paid pilots |
| **Claim a new name, "Owner Intelligence"** | Use the familiar "AI Chief of Staff" label | At least 11 products already use it; a contested label can't be owned (Section 00) | Yes — "works like a chief of staff" remains the explanation in sales conversations |

## What the plan asks investors to underwrite

Of the fourteen risks, three decide the outcome: **adoption speed and the March 2027 milestone (1, 2), category ownership (3) and India-hosted AI (4).** The first is answered by a date — fifty paid contracts by 31 March 2027 — before outside investors are asked to commit; the second by moving first and building India-specific depth; the third by a migration the plan commits to before taking any customer's money.

### Key Insights

- The decisive risk is the March 2027 milestone: with fifty paid contracts the business is self-funding; without them the previous plan needs ~₹6.0 Cr [[1]](#s11-ref-1).
- Frontier labs' personal agents are the fastest-moving competitive threat; Aroh's defence is organisation-level, India-specific depth [[4]](#s11-ref-4).
- AI processing outside India is a high-likelihood sales risk today, with a committed fix before the first paid contract [[6]](#s11-ref-6), [[5]](#s11-ref-5).
- IP and ownership between DEWiN and Hathi Labs must be documented before any outside round [[3]](#s11-ref-3).

### Investment Implications

The risks are concentrated in the first eighteen months and are measurable early: pilot behaviour, conversion, onboarding hours and the India-hosting migration all show up within the pre-launch period and FY2027–28. An investor is underwriting a short, well-instrumented period of uncertainty, after which the business either funds itself or shows clearly that it won't — with enough cash left to change course.
